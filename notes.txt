**Not settled yet — two gaps, one on each side.**

**ABM side — this is the real gap.** Whether "welfare insufficient" is mechanistically *explained* (not just narrated) depends on whether wealth compounding alone, absent reinvestment, can produce sustained rising inequality that redistribution merely dampens rather than reverses. Static compounding (interest accruing on a fixed wealth stock) tends toward an equilibrium Gini, not indefinite growth — it doesn't structurally explain a *persistent* rise. The standard mechanism that does is Piketty's r > g: capital income being reinvested into more capital (firms, real estate) faster than the economy/wages grow, which compounds *ownership share*, not just wealth level, and has no natural ceiling the way simple interest does. If your agents currently just accrue wealth passively rather than reinvesting it into productive/ownership assets that generate further income, you don't yet have a mechanism that can generate *sustained* Gini growth in the ABM — you'd likely see it plateau, which would undercut the very claim you want to make.

So: add the reinvestment channel. Practically, you don't need this fully general — you need enough to test one thing: run the ABM with redistribution on vs. off, both with reinvestment enabled, and check whether Gini trajectory is rising-but-dampened (supports "insufficient") vs. flat/falling (would undercut the claim) vs. rising identically regardless of redistribution (would suggest redistribution is irrelevant to the wealth-concentration channel entirely — also a real finding, just a different one).
This is a real, citable phenomenon in the welfare-targeting literature — categorical/cliff-based transfers concentrated on a fixed group can increase measured inequality relative to broad-based transfers, distinct from the transfer's size being wrong (Piven & Cloward's targeting-vs-universalism debate; more recent lumpy-transfer/benefit-cliff literature). But confirm it's actually happening before claiming it: check whether mean per-recipient subsidy in the ON condition grows across ticks, and whether Gini's rise is concentrated within the bottom quartile (recipients vs. non-recipient lower earners) rather than uniform. If that pattern holds, you have a genuine, interesting finding about transfer design, not a null about redistribution generally.
